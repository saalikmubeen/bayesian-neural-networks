\large A softness-controlled study of mean-field VI on MNIST}
\author{Saalik Mubeen\\Georgia Institute of Technology}
\date{October 2026}

\begin{abstract}
Bayesian neural networks are often said to detect out-of-distribution (OOD)
inputs better than deterministic networks. But their predictions are softer
everywhere, and a deterministic network can be softened for free by a
temperature. I compare each method with a deterministic network softened to
the same in-distribution entropy, in a series of pre-registered experiments
on MLPs trained on MNIST. At typical training budgets, mean-field Bayes by
Backprop (BBB) is no better than this softness-matched network. With longer
training it beats it by $+0.06$ to $+0.08$ AUROC. The advantage holds against
a plain network and a 5-member deep ensemble trained for the same number of
steps, and it is not reproduced by adding random weight noise to a trained
network, so it depends on the variances BBB learns. Among deterministic
detectors, a kNN detector on network features comes closest (within about
0.02 AUROC, and tying BBB in one setting). When this happens is set by
the optimizer, not the data. Under Adam the posterior standard deviations grow
on a clock of learning rate $\times$ steps: halving or doubling the learning rate
doubles or halves the time, while a 20-fold change in training-set size changes
it by at most 20\%. Under SGD the same growth depends strongly on the
training-set size, and where the standard deviations stay small BBB shows no
advantage at all. Which uncertainty score is reported also matters. With the
default $\mathcal N(0,1)$ prior, the entropy-based advantage can disappear later
in training as the model starts to underfit, while the mutual-information (MI)
advantage persists. With a prior of scale 0.3 both persist. MI is also high in
runs that never learned the task, so on its own it does not show a useful
model. The mechanism is specific. Long training leaves BBB effectively
overpruned: only 1--5\% of hidden weights carry more signal than noise, and the
advantage needs BBB's own means paired with its own allocation of noise.
OOD images drive the noisy first-layer weights much harder relative to their
signal, and a single analytic pass over the first layer's means and
variances reproduces BBB's sampled OOD detection within 0.02 AUROC, confirmed
on fresh models and two unseen OOD sets. A natural-gradient method (IVON)
escapes the optimizer clock, its variances scaling as $1/\sqrt{N}$, and shows no
advantage over the softness-matched network. On CIFAR-10 with small, underfit models (37--68\% accuracy across a CNN and
an MLP), no comparable advantage appears.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
A standard classifier gives a confident prediction for any input, including
inputs unlike its training data. Bayesian neural networks (BNNs) average
predictions over a distribution of weights \citep{blundell2015weight}, which
should raise uncertainty on unfamiliar inputs. The usual evidence is that a
BNN's predictive entropy is higher on OOD data than a deterministic network's.

That evidence has a confound. A BNN's averaged prediction is softer than a
deterministic network's on every input, and temperature scaling
\citep{guo2017calibration} softens a deterministic network at no cost.
Temperature is a known ingredient of OOD detection \citep{liang2018enhancing}
and appears as a baseline in uncertainty benchmarks \citep{ovadia2019can}.
Whether BNNs are intrinsically good OOD detectors has been questioned
\citep{henning2021bayesian}, and a Bayesian last layer is known to reduce
overconfidence far from the data \citep{kristiadi2020being}. The comparison I
use throughout is simple: each method against a deterministic network
softened, on validation data only, to that method's own in-distribution
entropy. The question is whether the method ranks OOD inputs as more
uncertain than in-distribution inputs better than a network of the same
softness.

\paragraph{Findings.}
\begin{enumerate}
\item At standard training budgets, mean-field BBB is no better than the
softness-matched network on MNIST $\to$ Fashion-MNIST and KMNIST. Deep
ensembles are slightly better; MC dropout ties; last-layer Laplace is worse
(\S\ref{sec:grid}).
\item With enough optimizer steps BBB beats the control whatever the
training-set size, and also beats step-matched plain networks and ensembles
and a weight-noise control; a kNN detector on features comes within about
0.02 and ties BBB in one setting
(\S\ref{sec:steps}, \S\ref{sec:mechanism}).
\item Under Adam, the posterior standard deviations grow on a learning-rate
$\times$ steps clock that is nearly independent of the training-set size; under
SGD it is not. No growth, no advantage (\S\ref{sec:clock}).
\item Along training, the entropy-based advantage can rise and fall while the
MI-based advantage persists. With a better-scaled prior both persist. MI is
also high for models that never learned (\S\ref{sec:window},
\S\ref{sec:prior}).
\item The advantage needs posterior noise in both hidden layers together, and
it is not a pixel shortcut (\S\ref{sec:mechanism}).
\item Long-trained BBB is effectively overpruned; the advantage needs its own
means paired with its own per-weight noise (\S\ref{sec:prune}). OOD inputs
separate in the first layer's noise-to-signal ratio, which reproduces BBB's
sampled OOD detection in one analytic pass (\S\ref{sec:r1}).
\item IVON, a natural-gradient variational method, follows $N$ rather than
the step count and has no advantage over the control; inflating its
variances does not create one (\S\ref{sec:ivon}).
\item Neither held-out NLL nor the ELBO picks a good stopping point. A
synthetic-outlier rule does no better than a fixed step count
(\S\ref{sec:select}).
\item On CIFAR-10 with small, underfit models, no comparable advantage
appears (\S\ref{sec:cifar}).
\end{enumerate}
Every experiment had its hypothesis, predictions and falsification criteria
written before it ran (committed to git for E1--E9; for E10--E18, SHA-256
hashes that I recorded before launch, which are self-attested rather than
independently timestamped). Appendix~\ref{app:ledger} lists all
predictions, including the many that failed.

\section{Setup}\label{sec:setup}
\paragraph{Data.} MNIST training subsets of $n\in\{500, 2{,}000, 10{,}000\}$
class-stratified images (one per seed); a fixed validation pool of 5{,}000
further training images, disjoint from every subset; the MNIST test set; OOD:
Fashion-MNIST \citep{xiao2017fashion} and KMNIST \citep{clanuwat2018deep} test
sets, normalised with MNIST statistics. CIFAR-10 experiments are described in
\S\ref{sec:cifar}.

\paragraph{Models.} All MNIST models use a 784--256--256--10 ReLU MLP.
\emph{DNN}: Adam ($10^{-3}$, cosine), 40 epochs, batch 64. \emph{MAP}: the
same network trained to the MAP estimate under BBB's $\mathcal N(0,1)$ prior,
with BBB's budget (150 epochs, gradient clipping). \emph{MC dropout}
\citep{gal2016dropout}: $p=0.1$, 50 samples. \emph{Deep ensemble}
\citep{lakshminarayanan2017simple}: 5 DNNs. \emph{Last-layer Laplace}
\citep{daxberger2021laplace}: full generalised Gauss--Newton covariance over
the last layer of a DNN, prior precision tuned on validation NLL.
\emph{IVON} \citep{shen2024variational}: the official implementation, with
effective sample size $N$ and weight decay $1/N$ (the same $\mathcal N(0,1)$
prior as BBB); learning rate and initial Hessian chosen once on a separate
subset by validation NLL. \emph{BBB}
\citep{blundell2015weight}: mean-field Gaussian posterior, $\mathcal N(0,1)$
prior, per-example negative ELBO
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L} = \frac{1}{B}\sum_{i\in\text{batch}} -\log p(y_i\mid x_i, w)
+ \frac{1}{N}\,\mathrm{KL}\big[q(w)\,\|\,p(w)\big], \qquad w=\mu+\sigma\odot\varepsilon,
\label{eq:elbo}
\end{equation}
with the KL weight warmed up linearly, $\sigma=\mathrm{softplus}(\rho)$ and
$\rho$ initialised at $-5$ ($\sigma_0\approx 0.007$), Adam $10^{-3}$, gradient
clipping, 50 posterior samples at test time. By default BBB trains for 150
epochs at $n\ge 2{,}000$ (4{,}800 steps at $n=2{,}000$) and the same number of
steps at $n=500$.

\paragraph{The softness-matched control.} For a method $M$, I fit a
temperature $T$ to a DNN's logits so that its mean predictive entropy on the
\emph{validation} set equals $M$'s. No test or OOD data enters the fit. The
reported quantity is
$\Delta = \mathrm{AUROC}(M) - \mathrm{AUROC}(\mathrm{DNN}_T)$, where AUROC
measures the separation of test from OOD images by predictive entropy.
$\Delta>0$ means $M$ ranks OOD inputs better than a network of the same
softness. The MI-based $\Delta$ uses $M$'s mutual-information AUROC minus the
same softness-matched DNN's entropy AUROC; a single deterministic network has
no mutual information, so this compares two different scores and should be
read as ``does $M$'s best epistemic score beat the softened network''.
Comparisons are paired by seed, with bootstrap confidence intervals and sign
counts (wins out of seeds). Wilcoxon tests are reported only with 10 seeds,
since with 5 seeds they cannot fall below $p=0.0625$. Unless stated otherwise,
BBB predictions use 50 posterior samples. The training-dynamics runs
(\S\ref{sec:window}--\S\ref{sec:select}) use 20; I did not compare the two
directly, so numbers from the two kinds of run are not interchangeable.

\subsection{The advantage follows optimizer steps, not data}\label{sec:steps}
With fixed epochs, a larger $n$ also means more optimizer steps: 23{,}550 at
$n=10{,}000$ vs 4{,}800 at $n\le 2{,}000$. Figure~\ref{fig:steps} decouples
the two. With the same data, 23{,}550 steps give $\Delta=+0.06$ to $+0.08$ in
5/5 seeds at every $n$, including $n=500$. 4{,}800 steps give no advantage,
including at $n=10{,}000$. Over this range the training-set size barely
matters for whether the advantage appears; \S\ref{sec:clock} shows why.

\subsection{Along training: entropy and MI diverge}\label{sec:window}
To see what happens in between, I trained BBB for 50{,}000 steps at
$n=2{,}000$ with a \emph{constant} learning rate and evaluated every 1{,}000
steps (Figure~\ref{fig:dyn}). With the default initialisation, the
entropy-based $\Delta$ on KMNIST is negative for the first $\sim$3{,}000 steps,
peaks at $+0.067$ to $+0.074$ around 9{,}000--14{,}000 steps (mean
first-layer $\sigma$ 0.65--0.82), and is negative again at 25{,}000--30{,}000
steps. The MI-based $\Delta$ does not fall: it is $+0.073$ at 10{,}000 steps,
$+0.071$ at 30{,}000 and $+0.061$ at 50{,}000. The entropy advantage disappears
because the model also becomes uncertain on in-distribution digits. Mean test
entropy rises from 0.30 to 0.94 as accuracy falls from 0.93 to 0.89. The
negative ELBO keeps improving throughout (143 at 4{,}000--5{,}000 steps, 6.8 at
24{,}000--25{,}000, 3.5 at 50{,}000). It drifts towards a posterior with
$\sigma\approx 0.98$ in the hidden layers (0.93 in the last), accuracy 0.89
and ECE 0.19. That is consistent with Coker et al.'s analysis of wide
mean-field BNNs \citep{coker2022wide}, whose theorem covers a different
setting (regression, odd activations). The entropy collapse is specific to
$n=2{,}000$: at 40{,}000 steps the entropy-based $\Delta$ is still $+0.033$
($n=500$) and $+0.060$ ($n=10{,}000$) on KMNIST.

Two cautions about MI. First, a slightly larger initial $\sigma$ (0.049)
reaches the advantage sooner (3{,}000 steps instead of 6{,}000), but
initialising $\sigma\ge 0.31$ prevents learning altogether: accuracy stays at
chance in a local optimum with a far worse ELBO (9.0--9.2 vs 3.4--3.5). These
chance-level models have the \emph{largest} MI-based $\Delta$ (about $+0.10$).
A positive MI $\Delta$ therefore does not by itself show a useful model. It
also reflects disagreement between near-prior posterior samples on any unusual
input. Second, these runs use a constant learning rate; the E4 runs
(\S\ref{sec:steps}) used a cosine schedule, which freezes training wherever the
budget ends.

\subsection{An optimizer clock}\label{sec:clock}
Why do steps matter more than data? For one weight with posterior
$\mathcal N(\mu,\sigma^2)$ and prior $\mathcal N(0,\sigma_p^2)$,
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{KL}=\log\frac{\sigma_p}{\sigma}+\frac{\sigma^2+\mu^2}{2\sigma_p^2}-\frac12,
\qquad
\frac{\partial\,\mathrm{KL}}{\partial\rho}
=\Big(\frac{\sigma}{\sigma_p^2}-\frac{1}{\sigma}\Big)\,\mathrm{sigmoid}(\rho)
\;\approx\;-1 \quad\text{for } \rho\ll 0,
\label{eq:klgrad}
\end{equation}
because $\sigma=\mathrm{softplus}(\rho)\approx\mathrm{sigmoid}(\rho)\approx e^{\rho}$
when $\rho\ll0$. In Eq.~\ref{eq:elbo} this gradient is weighted by $1/N$, and
while $\sigma$ is tiny the likelihood term's gradient on $\rho$ (which scales
with $\sigma$) is negligible. So the gradient on each $\rho$ is roughly the
constant $-1/N$. Adam divides each gradient by a running estimate of its
magnitude, so a gradient of constant sign produces a step of about the
learning rate $\eta$, independently of its size and hence of $N$; this is
Adam's known sign-descent behaviour \citep{balles2018dissecting}, applied
here to the variance parameters. Hence
\begin{equation}
t^\star \approx \frac{\rho^\star-\rho_0}{\eta},
\label{eq:clock}
\end{equation}
the number of steps for $\rho$ to travel from $\rho_0$ to a target $\rho^\star$.
For $\sigma=0.3$ ($\rho^\star=-1.05$) from $\rho_0=-5$ this gives $t^\star\approx
3{,}950$ steps at $\eta=10^{-3}$. Under SGD the step is instead $\eta_\rho/N$
(times $1/(1-\beta)$ with momentum $\beta$), so the same growth takes $N$ times
longer as $N$ grows. The approximation breaks down once $\sigma$ is no longer
small compared with the prior, or once the likelihood term pulls back. Unlu
and Aitchison \citep{unlu2020variational} observe that mean-field variance
parameters are often unconverged when training stops, and Nishida and Taiji
\citep{nishida2022adamb} report that BBB's variational parameters grow when it
is trained with Adam. Eq.~\ref{eq:clock} makes that growth quantitative. A first hint was already in the E9 runs (Adam,
learning rate $10^{-3}$): the mean first-layer $\rho$ was $-3.54$, $-3.52$ and
$-3.62$ after 1{,}000 steps, $-1.88$, $-1.85$ and $-1.92$ after 3{,}000, and
$-1.01$, $-0.99$ and $-1.09$ after 5{,}000, for $n = 500$, $2{,}000$ and
$10{,}000$.

I then tested this directly (Figure~\ref{fig:clock}; 3 seeds per arm, 40{,}000
steps, $n\in\{500, 10{,}000\}$). With Adam, the time for the mean first-layer
$\sigma$ to reach 0.3 was 10{,}000, 5{,}000 and 3{,}000 steps at $n=500$ and
12{,}000, 6{,}000 and 3{,}000 at $n=10{,}000$, for learning rates
$5\times10^{-4}$, $10^{-3}$ and $2\times10^{-3}$. Time $\times$ learning rate was
constant to within 12\%, and changing $n$ twenty-fold changed it by at most
20\%. Eq.~\ref{eq:clock} predicts 7{,}900, 3{,}950 and 1{,}975 steps with no
fitted constant: it gets the $1/\eta$ scaling and the independence from $n$
right, and the observed times are 1.27--1.52 times longer, consistent with
gradient noise making Adam's steps somewhat smaller than $\eta$. At
$\eta=10^{-3}$ the gap is larger at $n=10{,}000$ (1.52) than at $n=500$ (1.27),
which fits that explanation: the constant $1/N$ signal is smaller relative to
the minibatch noise when $N$ is larger. These times are measured on a 1{,}000-step grid, which at the highest
learning rate (3{,}000 steps) is coarser than the 12\% figure; and the
$n=500$, $2\times10^{-3}$ arm collapsed later in training (see below). The onset of the OOD advantage followed the same clock (onset $\times$
learning rate constant to within 7\% at $n=10{,}000$). With SGD (momentum 0.9)
on the $\rho$ parameters, the mean $\sigma$ after 20{,}000 steps was 0.67 at
$n=500$ but 0.017 at $n=10{,}000$, a 39-fold difference. In that last setting
$\sigma$ never grew, and BBB had \emph{no} OOD advantage (peak $\Delta$ $-0.007$)
while reaching the best accuracy (0.966). At $n=500$, SGD grew $\sigma$ to 0.67 by
20{,}000 steps but gave only a small advantage (peak $\Delta$ on KMNIST $+0.03$,
never reaching the $+0.04$ onset criterion; accuracy 0.882). All four E10
predictions held; the follow-up below (E13) did less well. Two side observations: at $n=500$ a learning rate of $2\times10^{-3}$
collapsed training (final accuracy 0.09--0.67), and plotted against learning
rate $\times$ steps the Adam curves coincide during growth, with $n$ only
shifting the plateau slightly.

In these runs gradient clipping acted on all parameters jointly, so under SGD
the $\rho$ step also depended on the cross-entropy gradient. I therefore
repeated the SGD arm with clipping on the means only (3 seeds per arm). The
dependence on $n$ remained. At $n=500$, $\sigma$ reached 0.3 by 5{,}000 steps in
every seed; at $n=10{,}000$ it never did ($\sigma=0.018$ at 20{,}000 steps,
against 0.945 at $n=500$ in the one seed that ran past 20{,}000 steps; the other
two diverged first, see below). The SGD form of Eq.~\ref{eq:clock} predicts
these numbers with no fitted constant. With $\eta_\rho=0.05$ and momentum 0.9
the step on $\rho$ is about $0.05\cdot10/N$, i.e.\ $5\times10^{-5}$ at
$n=10{,}000$. After 20{,}000 steps (less half of the 640-step KL warm-up) that
gives $\rho\approx-4.02$ and $\sigma\approx0.018$, against 0.0177 observed (0.0173 in
E10). At $n=500$ the step is $10^{-3}$, so $\sigma$ should reach 0.3 after about
4{,}300 steps; the first checkpoint at or above 0.3 was 5{,}000. Without clipping, the $n=500$ runs also reached a larger
advantage ($+0.057$ to $+0.060$), so the small $+0.03$ above was partly the
clipping slowing $\sigma$ down. Scaling the $\rho$ learning rate with $N$ (1.0 at
$n=10{,}000$) made $\sigma$ reach 0.3 at the same checkpoint as at $n=500$
(5{,}000) in two of three seeds, but both crossed shortly before diverging
(at 6{,}000 and 9{,}000 steps) and the third diverged first, so this is weak
evidence. But
unclipped SGD on $\rho$ turned out to be unstable: it diverged in 6 of 9 runs,
every run in which $\sigma$ grew large (at 17{,}000--34{,}000 steps for $n=500$,
and 5{,}000--9{,}000 for the scaled runs). The scaled runs therefore stopped
before any OOD advantage could appear (peak $+0.022$). Two of the three
pre-registered predictions of this follow-up failed or could not be scored as
stated.

\subsection{Prior scale}\label{sec:prior}
The $\mathcal N(0,1)$ prior is badly misspecified for layers whose weights have
scale $\sim0.05$ \citep{fortuin2022priors}. I reran the dynamics at $n=2{,}000$
with priors of scale 0.1 and 0.3 (3 seeds, 40{,}000 steps). The MI-based
advantage survived both: its peak on KMNIST was $+0.055$ ($\sigma_p=0.1$) and
$+0.075$ ($\sigma_p=0.3$), against $+0.080$ for $\sigma_p=1$. With $\sigma_p=0.3$
the posterior $\sigma$ reached the prior scale by about 10{,}000 steps and stayed
there, and the entropy-based advantage no longer disappeared: at 40{,}000 steps
it was $+0.045$ (KMNIST) and $+0.048$ (Fashion-MNIST), against $-0.009$ and
$+0.015$ for $\sigma_p=1$. Much of the entropy collapse in \S\ref{sec:window} is
therefore $\sigma$ drifting all the way to an oversized prior. A tighter prior
did not help fit, contrary to my prediction: with $\sigma_p=0.1$ accuracy was
lower (0.870 vs 0.896), because the prior also pulls the weight means towards
zero, and the Fashion-MNIST advantage mostly vanished.

\subsection{What produces the advantage}\label{sec:mechanism}
\paragraph{Noise in both hidden layers.} Taking BBB models trained for 23{,}550 steps,
I switched posterior noise off layer by layer. Noise in the first and second
layers together keeps 72--82\% of $\Delta$. Noise in any single layer keeps
essentially none (first layer: 16\% on Fashion-MNIST, $-26\%$ on KMNIST).
The posterior-mean network on its own is a worse OOD detector than the DNN.
The advantage comes from noise compounding across the hidden layers. Noise
in the last layer alone does not reproduce it in this model, which may explain
why last-layer Laplace did poorly in Table~\ref{tab:grid}.

\paragraph{Not a weak baseline, and not just noise.} I compared BBB trained
for 23{,}550 steps with stronger controls (5 seeds; $n\in\{2{,}000,
10{,}000\}$; both OOD sets; Table~\ref{tab:controls}).
\emph{Weight noise:} adding Gaussian noise to every layer of a trained plain
network (scaled to each layer's weight spread and matched to BBB's
validation entropy) gave AUROC 0.81--0.89, and BBB beat it by $+0.10$ to
$+0.15$. Even the best noise scale chosen with knowledge of the OOD set
stayed $+0.07$ to $+0.10$ below BBB. So the advantage is not produced by
\emph{any} weight noise; it depends on the variances BBB learns.
\emph{Matched training budgets:} a plain network and a 5-member ensemble
trained for the same 23{,}550 steps stayed below BBB in all 5 seeds in every
setting ($+0.040$ to $+0.087$ against the softened network, $+0.041$ to $+0.075$
against the ensemble). \emph{Other scores:} on the plain networks, maximum
softmax probability, maximum logit, energy and an oracle-tuned temperature
reached at most 0.888--0.918 on average, and a Mahalanobis detector on
penultimate features 0.86--0.94. A kNN detector on normalised features came
closest, 0.93--0.97, within $+0.006$ to $+0.023$ of BBB; at $n=10{,}000$ on
Fashion-MNIST it ties BBB (2/5 seeds better).

\paragraph{Not a pixel shortcut.} Many pixels are constant (always
background) across a training subset: on average 192, 148 and 104 at
$n=500$, $2{,}000$ and $10{,}000$. A model-free rule, ``flag
any image with ink on such a pixel'', separates MNIST from Fashion-MNIST and
KMNIST with AUROC 0.967--1.000 over 15 training subsets (means
0.990--1.000), a real weakness of these benchmarks. But the
models do not rely on it. Resetting those pixels to background in the OOD
images leaves every method's AUROC and $\Delta$ essentially unchanged (BBB at
$n=10{,}000$: $+0.057\to+0.055$ and $+0.070\to+0.070$). The growth of
$\sigma$ is also global, not concentrated on those pixels (ratio 1.0--1.24).

\subsection{An overpruned posterior}\label{sec:prune}
What do BBB's long-trained variances look like? They are far from uniform
(Figure~\ref{fig:prune}). In the two hidden layers the median $\sigma$ is
0.71--0.84, close to the prior, and only 1.2--4.5\% of weights have $|\mu|>\sigma$.
In the second layer $|\mu|$ and $\sigma$ are strongly anti-correlated ($-0.64$ to
$-0.72$), and the last layer is nearly deterministic. This is the overpruning
described by Trippe and Turner \citep{trippe2018overpruning}: a small, precise
subnetwork computes the prediction and most other weights carry prior-scale
noise. A pre-registered swap test shows that both halves of this pairing
matter. Putting BBB's variances on the means of a plain network trained for
the same number of steps collapses it to 13--15\% accuracy. Keeping BBB's means
but shuffling its variances within each layer cuts accuracy to 0.81 and 0.59
and turns the OOD advantage negative ($-0.16$ to $-0.32$). I had predicted that
shuffling would not matter, because the \emph{average} variance on always-blank
and active pixels was similar (\S\ref{sec:mechanism}); that summary hid the
per-weight structure.

\subsection{Where OOD inputs separate: the first layer}\label{sec:r1}
For an input $x$, the first layer's mean pre-activation is
$m_j=\sum_i\mu_{ij}x_i+\mu_{b_j}$ and the variance that posterior noise adds to it
is $v_j=\sum_i\sigma_{ij}^2x_i^2+\sigma_{b_j}^2$. Their ratio,
\begin{equation}
R_1(x)=\frac{\sum_j v_j}{\sum_j m_j^2},
\label{eq:r1}
\end{equation}
measures how much noise an input routes through the first layer relative to
the signal it produces. I had pre-registered the same ratio for the
\emph{second} layer, computed from the mean first-layer activations, as the
place where in- and out-of-distribution inputs separate; that prediction failed
(AUROC 0.45--0.69), partly because it ignores noise passed on from the first
layer. The first-layer ratio instead matched BBB's own detection. I then
confirmed this in a separate pre-registered test on ten freshly trained BBB
models and on two OOD sets never used before, grayscale CIFAR-10 and SVHN
(Figure~\ref{fig:r1}). On all four far-OOD sets $R_1$ reached AUROC
0.960--0.991, within 0.003--0.019 of BBB's 50-sample mutual information, and
correlated with BBB's per-image MI (Spearman 0.71--0.83). In-distribution
digits are computed through the low-noise subnetwork; OOD images also drive
high-variance weights. Second-layer noise is then needed to turn this into
disagreement between posterior samples (\S\ref{sec:mechanism}). The account is
incomplete for near-OOD inputs: on digits rotated by 90° at $n=10{,}000$, BBB
reached 0.950 and $R_1$ only 0.863, so deeper layers add information there. A
practical side effect is that $R_1$ is a sampling-free OOD score for a trained
BBB network.

\subsection{Natural-gradient VI does not show the effect}\label{sec:ivon}
IVON \citep{shen2024variational} keeps a Gaussian posterior
$\mathcal N(m, 1/(N(h+\delta)))$ whose variance is set by the training-set size and
a running estimate $h$ of the loss curvature, not by an Adam step count. In a
pre-registered comparison (Figure~\ref{fig:ivon}; 3 seeds per $n$, 40{,}000
steps), IVON's mean first-layer $\sigma$ was 0.170, 0.086 and 0.039 at $n=500$,
$2{,}000$ and $10{,}000$: the $n=500$ to $n=10{,}000$ ratio of 4.41 is close to
the $1/\sqrt{N}$ value of 4.47, and the variances stayed far below the prior
instead of drifting to it as BBB's did (0.96--0.98). At $n\ge2{,}000$ IVON was as
accurate as the plain network (0.930 and 0.969) and its OOD behaviour did not
change with training length. But it had no advantage over the
softness-matched network ($\Delta$ peaks of $-0.005$ and $-0.008$; FPR95 no
better), against BBB's $+0.07$ and $+0.065$. At $n=500$ IVON was less accurate
than the plain network and its $\Delta$ drifted down. To test whether
variance size alone explains BBB's advantage, I then inflated IVON's
variances by tempering the likelihood ($p(D\mid w)^{1/k}p(w)$, $k\in\{10, 100\}$,
same prior). This raised $\sigma$ by 3.1--9.3$\times$, but no tempered model gained
an advantage: the only one that still learned ($n=10{,}000$, $k=10$, accuracy
0.951) stayed at $-0.004$, and the others collapsed to chance accuracy. Together
with the weight-noise control and the swap test, this points to the specific
pairing BBB learns, not to the size of its variances. One caveat: IVON's
learning rate and initial Hessian were tuned for $k=1$ and kept fixed, so the
collapses may partly reflect settings unsuited to tempering.

\subsection{Choosing when to stop}\label{sec:select}
Neither in-distribution criterion picks a good checkpoint. Minimum held-out
NLL stops at 1{,}000--3{,}000 steps, before the advantage appears; minimum
$-$ELBO stops at the end of training. I tested a rule that needs no real OOD
data. I built three synthetic outlier sets from the validation images
(uniform noise, flipped digits, pixel-permuted digits) and picked the
checkpoint that best separates validation images from them by MI
(Table~\ref{tab:select}; 12 runs, $n\in\{500, 2{,}000, 10{,}000\}$). It reaches
74\% of the best achievable entropy-based advantage (the ``oracle''), but
simply stopping at a fixed 8{,}000 steps reaches 72\%. Given the clock in
\S\ref{sec:clock}, a fixed step count is a sensible rule for a fixed learning
rate. Scoring the last
checkpoint by MI instead of entropy, with no selection at all, does as well
($+0.049$). The oracle is a maximum over 40 checkpoints and is optimistic. These
runs also share configurations and seeds with \S\ref{sec:window}. The proxy
rule therefore does not beat trivial alternatives here.

\section{CIFAR-10}\label{sec:cifar}
I repeated the dynamics experiment on CIFAR-10 ($n=10{,}000$, no
augmentation) with a small CNN (three $3\times3$ conv layers of 32, 64 and
128 channels, then 256 and 10 units), every layer Bayesian. OOD sets were SVHN
(10{,}000 images) and CIFAR-100. No comparable advantage appeared (Figure~\ref{fig:cifar}).
BBB's peak $\Delta$ on SVHN was $-0.044$, $+0.006$ and $-0.056$ in the three
seeds, and on CIFAR-100 at most $+0.031$, early in training. Even a 5-member
deep ensemble lost to a single softness-matched DNN on SVHN (AUROC
0.727--0.740 vs 0.759--0.807), while winning on CIFAR-100 by $+0.02$ to $+0.03$.
Scored by MI instead, BBB was \emph{worse} on SVHN: MI-AUROC ranged from 0.24
to 0.60 across checkpoints, often below chance. The drift towards the prior
did replicate. The large fully connected layer's
$\sigma$ reached 0.996, the ELBO kept improving, and accuracy fell from
0.67--0.68 to 0.61--0.63. The first conv layer's $\sigma$ stayed small (0.04).

To separate architecture from data, I trained the MNIST study's MLP on
flattened CIFAR-10 images at $n=2{,}000$ and $10{,}000$ (3 seeds each). Its
deterministic version reaches 38\% and 46\% test accuracy. BBB again showed no
advantage on SVHN (peak $\Delta$ $-0.031$ to $+0.030$). On CIFAR-100 it gained
$+0.030$ to $+0.046$ for the first few thousand steps, similar to the CNN's
$+0.024$ to $+0.031$. Then it collapsed towards the prior (first-layer
$\sigma\to0.997$--$0.999$, accuracy falling to 0.10--0.29). Much larger
CIFAR-100 ``gains'' at $n=2{,}000$ ($+0.09$ to $+0.21$) appear only after
collapse, at near-chance accuracy, and are not meaningful. My pre-registered
decision rule counted these runs as support for an architectural
explanation, but it had no accuracy condition, and the qualifying gains are
barely above its threshold and no larger than the CNN's. On natural images,
neither architecture shows the MNIST-sized advantage ($+0.06$ to $+0.08$ on both
OOD sets). The evidence points more to the data, or to the much harder task
(37--47\% accuracy), than to the CNN's structure.

\section{Related work}
Coker et al.\ \citep{coker2022wide} prove that the optimal mean-field
posterior predictive approaches the prior predictive as width grows; the drift
in \S\ref{sec:window} is consistent with that effect along the optimisation
path of a standard-width ReLU classifier, a setting their theorem does not cover. Unlu and Aitchison \citep{unlu2020variational} note
that mean-field variance parameters are often unconverged when training stops
and recommend a larger learning rate for them; Nishida and Taiji
\citep{nishida2022adamb} report that BBB's variational parameters grow under
Adam and propose a decoupled optimizer (AdamB). \S\ref{sec:clock} derives
the $N$-independent clock behind this growth and links it to when BBB's OOD
advantage appears. In natural-gradient variational methods such as Vadam
\citep{khan2018fast} and IVON \citep{shen2024variational} the variances are
tied to $N$ and the loss curvature by construction; \S\ref{sec:ivon} confirms
that IVON does not follow the clock, and also shows that it does not reproduce
BBB's OOD advantage. Foong et al.\
\citep{foong2020expressiveness} show what mean-field and MC dropout
approximations cannot represent. Wenzel et al.\ \citep{wenzel2020good} show
that tempered (``cold'') posteriors can predict better than the Bayes
posterior; the softness-matched control here asks a related question about
temperature, for OOD detection. Depeweg et al.\ \citep{depeweg2018decomposition}
decompose predictive uncertainty into aleatoric and epistemic (MI) parts, the
split behind \S\ref{sec:window}; Wimmer et al.\ \citep{wimmer2023quantifying}
question whether conditional entropy and MI are appropriate measures of these
parts, which fits the caution about MI in \S\ref{sec:window}. Shen et al.\ \citep{shen2024variational}
show that a natural-gradient variational method (IVON) trains large networks
as well as Adam; whether its variances follow a similar clock is open. Izmailov et al.\
\citep{izmailov2021dangers} show that weights on always-zero pixels keep their
prior under HMC and hurt accuracy under covariate shift; I find no such
pixel-specific effect in BBB's OOD detection. Ovadia et al.\
\citep{ovadia2019can} include temperature-scaled baselines and find ensembles
strongest under shift; Ashukha et al.\ \citep{ashukha2020pitfalls} argue that
uncertainty methods should be compared after temperature scaling, which the
softness-matched control extends to OOD detection; and Abe et al.\
\citep{abe2022deep} find that ensembles' OOD gains are largely explained by
their in-distribution performance. Odgers et al.\ \citep{odgers2026gaussian} show that mean-field VI can
overestimate predictive variance on in-distribution points, which matches the
rising in-distribution entropy in \S\ref{sec:window}. Trippe and Turner
\citep{trippe2018overpruning} describe overpruning, where variational BNNs
switch off units by sending their weights to the prior; \S\ref{sec:prune}
finds the same structure in long-trained BBB and links it to OOD detection. Fortuin et al.\
\citep{fortuin2022priors} show that isotropic Gaussian priors are often poor
choices for neural networks. Henning et al.\ \citep{henning2021bayesian} question
whether exact BNN posteriors are intrinsically good OOD detectors. Raina and
Schmah \citep{raina2025ood} report Bayesian advantages for OOD detection from
small training sets; the results here suggest checking training length and
softness before attributing such advantages to the posterior.

\section{Limitations}
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Scope.} The positive results come from small MLPs on
MNIST-family data. The CIFAR-10 models were small and underfit (37--68\%
accuracy, no augmentation), so the CIFAR result says little about well-trained
networks.
\item \textbf{Prior.} Only three prior scales (0.1, 0.3, 1) at one
training-set size; per-layer or fan-in-scaled priors were not tried.
\item \textbf{Schedule and samples.} The dynamics runs use a constant learning
rate and 20 posterior samples; the fixed-budget runs use a cosine schedule and
50 samples.
\item \textbf{Statistics.} Dynamics results rest on 3 seeds per setting, and the
selection analysis reuses those configurations and seeds.
\item \textbf{The clock.} Tested with Adam and SGD on one architecture; other
adaptive optimizers and natural-gradient methods such as IVON
\citep{shen2024variational} were not tried. Unclipped SGD on $\rho$ diverged
whenever $\sigma$ grew large, so the SGD contrast rests on the stable phase.
\item \textbf{Baselines.} All methods use the same small MLP with default
hyperparameters, without per-method tuning. A kNN feature detector comes within
0.02 of BBB and ties it in one setting.
\item \textbf{Mechanism.} The first-layer account was found in an
exploratory analysis and then confirmed in a separate pre-registered test; it
does not cover near-OOD inputs. IVON was run with one tuned setting, kept
fixed when tempering.
\item \textbf{No exact posterior.} Without HMC or similar, I cannot say whether
any of this reflects Bayesian behaviour or only mean-field VI under Adam.
\end{itemize}

\section{Conclusion}
For Bayes by Backprop on MNIST-family data, ``knowing what it doesn't know''
is not a fixed property of the method. It is set by optimizer time. Under
Adam the posterior variances grow on a learning-rate $\times$ steps clock,
almost regardless of how much data there is, and the OOD advantage switches on
with them. Where the variances stay small, as under SGD with more data, there
is no advantage. At typical budgets BBB therefore behaves like a
temperature-scaled network; with longer training it beats the
softness-matched control, step-matched plain networks and ensembles, and a
weight-noise control, with a kNN feature detector close behind. The uncertainty score and the prior scale then decide whether
the advantage lasts. Mechanistically, long training leaves BBB overpruned, and
OOD inputs route disproportionate noise through its first layer; a
natural-gradient method that ties its variances to the data instead shows no
such advantage. Comparisons between BBB and other methods should report
the optimizer, learning rate and training budget, the score, and a
softness-matched baseline.

\section{KL scaling}\label{app:kl}
Dividing the KL term in Eq.~\ref{eq:elbo} by the number of mini-batches
instead of by $N$ is an easy slip when the cross-entropy is averaged per
batch. It over-weights the KL term by $N/\lceil N/B\rceil\approx B$ (62.5$\times$
at $N=2{,}000$, $B=64$). In a 3-seed pilot it cost BBB 3.2 points of accuracy
(0.904 vs 0.936) and raised ECE from 0.022 to 0.185.
